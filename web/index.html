How a warning gets out

Four layers between the model and a phone.

The model only proposes text. Code, reviewers and people each get a say, and every step is written to a hash-chained log that anyone can verify.

1. Drafter and translator

One agent turns verified inputs into CAP fields and leaves unverified ones out. A second writes the Arabic, from a vetted template when one exists, and flags machine translation for review.

2. Twelve fixed rules

Rules R0 to R11 are plain code. Each quotes a public policy or an official investigation. A failed rule blocks the alert; a missing approval holds it.

3. Four reviewer agents

Met office, issuing authority, network operator and regulator, and an Arabic reviewer. Each sees only its part, searches 11,572 clauses (UAE texts first) and must quote its source word for word, or the objection is dropped.

4. Named people

A severe alert needs two approvers, plus a named reader for machine-translated Arabic. Their roles and the AI's part are written into the alert, which is then signed.

Policy and standards gaps

Seven gaps the mapping turned up.

Each one has sources and a global example in the repository. We also searched the organisers' UAE policy dataset, 62 documents clause by clause, for each gap; the closest clauses are about other things.

G1 · standards (ITU-T SG17)

CAP has no field for AI involvement or a named approver.

In 1,711 live alerts from 8 feeds, none records either. We propose five registered parameters, signed with the alert. A draft profile is in the repository.

G2 · UAE policy

No public rule on who approves an AI-drafted public alert.

The UAE AI Charter states principles only. Global example: the FCC's two-person recommendation after Hawaii.

G3 · UAE policy

No rule on machine translation of alerts.

The US is split: the FCC refuses it for phone alerts, while the National Weather Service uses AI translation.

G4 · UAE policy

No public CAP feed on the WMO register.

The UAE record is dated 2013. Saudi Arabia, Qatar and Kuwait publish live feeds.

G5 · international norm

No norm on correcting a false alert.

3 of 1,000 recent US alerts cancelled earlier alerts without linking to them.

G6 · standards

Vetted templates are sized for a 360-character US limit, not cell broadcast pages.

The FCC's Arabic dust storm template runs to 10 pages in UCS-2. No UAE text sets a page budget, and TDRA TS FS 001 v2.0 sets none.

G7 · standards

CAP status and US test practice disagree.

246 of 1,000 US alerts marked "Actual" carry a test event code.

Limits

What this does not prove.

Are the replays the original systems?

No. Each one is rebuilt from public reports. Where the record is silent, we assume the alert was fine. Four incidents rest on official statements rather than investigation reports, and the India case on press reports only.

Who wrote the faults and answer keys?

The 30-fault set and its matching terms are ours. The 24 pre-registered faults were written by a separate agent that saw only the drafts, and committed before the run. The replays use the investigators' own findings as the answer key.

Are the reviewer agents the real institutions?

No. They stand in for roles. Two models play the six agents: one drafts and translates, another reviews.

What about the UAE specifics?

The issuer register, target zones and signing keys are demo stand-ins. TDRA's 2019 UAE-Alert specification is the primary text for handset behaviour; the 2015 handset rules come from a compliance firm's summary.

Does the Taiwan miss matter?

Yes. The frozen rules missed it because R11 only checks AI-written text, and Taiwan's wrong English came from an old human template. The met office reviewer caught it. A grounding check should run on every alert.
